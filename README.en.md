## Introduction

Over the past few years, my wife and I went through the process of working with a US surrogate several times. Through surrogacy, we were blessed with a very healthy boy and a very healthy girl. We are very, very happy.

After hearing about our experience, many friends asked us about the details, both friends in China and friends here in the US. Talking with them, we could feel that more and more families are struggling to balance having children with their careers. It's a new problem for a new generation, one that comes with rising productivity and a deeper commitment to gender equality.

We have walked this road ourselves. We also know that surrogacy is a relatively new field that involves many sensitive personal choices, and overall there is very little reliable information online. We did a lot of homework and took quite a few wrong turns. We'd like to share what we learned, in the hope that it helps other intended parents. Thanks also to my good friend K for his contribution, which covers the process for a gay male couple using egg donation and surrogacy in the US.

**Disclaimer:** **This guide involves no commercial interests** and **does not recommend any clinic or agency**.

Having been through the process several times, we understand that outcomes vary a lot from person to person. A clinic or protocol that didn't give us the result we wanted may work perfectly well for someone else, and vice versa.

Feel free to start a discussion in the issues. Other parents who have been through something similar are very welcome to share their advice and experience by submitting a pull request.


## Why Surrogacy

Everyone's situation and reasons are different. Some couples have trouble conceiving (which can happen to either partner), some are same-sex couples, and some people want to have and raise a child on their own.

For us, the core reason was my wife's career. She is passionate about her work and was on the rise in her career. At that stage, ten months of pregnancy during which it's hard to function, followed by roughly a year of breastfeeding every few hours through the night — two years like that would have made it impossible for our little family to balance life and work, no matter how we divided the labor or how much help we got from our parents. So we chose surrogacy. Because China determines parentage by birth rather than by DNA, we chose to come to California.

Looking back, compared with most of our friends who had children naturally, our journey was easier. At every stage of the pregnancy and the newborn period, my wife was able to keep working with full focus and high performance, and to keep finding joy in life. Before our kids were born, we did worry whether, without having gone through the hormonal changes of pregnancy, we might not feel a strong emotional bond with them. But in fact, we have loved our babies from the very first look.

Another thing I find interesting: because we didn't share the ups and downs of pregnancy with our kids, our attitude toward raising them is fairly rational. We rarely panic the moment they cry. Along the same lines, our parenting philosophy right now is: **adults' life decisions come first, and the kids come along with us.**

Of course, I fully understand that having children is a sacred topic for everyone. Some friends think we are irresponsible parents; others think surrogacy exploits or objectifies other women. We also ask ourselves whether we regret not experiencing pregnancy firsthand. But like most things in life, there is no perfect choice — we can only choose what's best for us. If this choice offends you, I'm sorry. But as a husband, my single most important decision-making principle is: **listen to my wife.**


## What is Surrogacy

Reproduction starts when a woman's egg and a man's sperm combine to form an embryo (a fertilized egg). The embryo grows inside a woman's body for 30–40 weeks, and then the baby is delivered. There are two key milestones here: forming the embryo, and delivering the baby.

In surrogacy, the woman's eggs are retrieved surgically and combined with the man's sperm in a lab to grow embryos (this part of the process is called In Vitro Fertilization, IVF). From there, there are two options: transfer the embryo back into the mother who provided the egg, so she carries the pregnancy herself; or transfer the embryo into another woman, who carries the pregnancy on her behalf. The surrogacy we discuss here is mainly the latter.

| | **Embryo (fertilized egg)** | **Delivery** |
|---|---|---|
| Natural | Egg and sperm meet inside the mother's body through intercourse | The embryo develops inside the mother, who gives birth naturally |
| Surrogacy | The mother's eggs are retrieved surgically and the father's sperm is collected; they are combined and cultured in a lab | A. The embryo can be transferred back into the mother, who gives birth naturally (IVF)<br>B. The embryo is transferred into another woman, who carries the pregnancy on her behalf (this is what "surrogacy" mainly refers to in this guide) |

A common concern in this process is the effect of egg retrieval on the woman. Some literature discusses this:

Medications called Gonadotropins (Gonal-F, Follistim, and Menopur) may have side effects including a situation where the ovaries become over-stimulated, leading to a condition called Ovarian Hyperstimulation Syndrome ("OHSS"), which is more likely to occur on women with high AMH. In the most severe form of OHSS, serious complications may result, which may require hospitalization and medical intervention. Other reported complications, while rare, include ovarian torsion (twisting of the ovary and potential loss of ovary), cyst formation, blood clots, kidney failure, fluid overload, and death.

We went through many rounds of discussion with different doctors ourselves. Eggs develop from follicles, and the number of antral follicles is a natural trait of each woman — some have many (20–30), some have few (10); it varies from person to person. **Egg retrieval does not use up a woman's future eggs.** It simply lets the antral follicles all develop during the same cycle.

For any given woman, follicle count declines over time. A woman has fewer antral follicles at 35 than at 30, and fewer at 30 than at 25. From this perspective:

1) **If you plan to use surrogacy, the earlier you start, the better.** The later you start, the lower a woman's antral follicle count, and the antral follicle count is the starting point of everything else. Everyone's biological clock is a bit different, but after 35 a woman's follicle count starts to drop, and the rate of DNA abnormalities in both partners' reproductive cells rises sharply, so the chance of creating a healthy embryo falls dramatically. These two reasons are why people often say "fertility falls off a cliff after 35."

2) If you're a woman who isn't absolutely committed to never having children, **add the six-hormone panel plus an AMH test to your regular checkups**. Tracking your body's biological clock lets you estimate how much runway you have left for having children, so you can plan in the way that's best for you.

## How Does Surrogacy Work

I define the complete surrogacy process as running from choosing a clinic to successfully returning to China with the baby 2–3 months after birth. There are several major stages: pre-screening checkup → clinic → embryos → surrogate → newborn. Each stage has both an operational side and a legal side. A word of warning up front: the legal side is very important. But it's also quite complicated, so I won't cover it here for now. If there's demand, I'll add it later.


### A. Pre-screening Medical Checkup

We recommend that both partners (if applicable) complete a pre-screening checkup first. Polycystic ovary syndrome, abnormal hormone levels, HPV infection, and chlamydia infection are all common issues that must be treated first; otherwise they may delay the rest of the process.

### B. The Clinic

The clinic is the core of the whole process. For most first-timers, it's a process with huge information asymmetry (the doctor knows far too much, and you have no idea what you should know) and uncertain outcomes (this is not a precise experimental science). Most of the friends I know went through at least two cycles.

We did a cycle at a very famous clinic (Elon Musk has used it). Some of our friends love that clinic and got great results. Our experience wasn't as good, though. When we finally asked the doctor, politely, why the results weren't what we'd hoped for, he shrugged and said: "I'm sorry but it happens." We were extremely frustrated at the time, because we had no idea what we could do better the second time, and we even started to wonder whether something was wrong with my wife or me. Fortunately, we later found a clinic that communicated transparently and was willing to share information with us, and we eventually got the result we wanted.

So it's important to find a clinic with a good track record, that pays close attention to you, and that communicates well.

In my view, finding a clinic essentially comes down to referrals from people you know plus screening the data. The US CDC requires every IVF clinic to report its success rates each year. These reports are actually very detailed, broken down by patient age group and various post-procedure success rates, and you can build your own multi-variable pivot tables:

[https://www.cdc.gov/art/artdata/index.html](https://www.cdc.gov/art/artdata/index.html)

But this official data has two problems. First, it's self-reported, so some clinics will always exaggerate. Second, there's selection bias: if a clinic wants its numbers to look good, it's likely to turn away more complicated patients (say, women over 35) to protect its success rate. Some of our friends chose clinics purely based on the data analysis, and their experience wasn't as good as they expected. In the end, most people still rely on word of mouth and go with a clinic recommended by friends.

Another organization closely tied to the clinic is the **lab**. The clinic handles the hormone protocol, pre-procedure monitoring, and the actual egg retrieval; the lab handles culturing the embryos. The lab generally doesn't deal with patients directly, so it's hard to talk to the people running it. But they have a critical effect on your outcome. Even if you retrieve a lot of eggs, one slip in the lab can cost you half of them. When choosing a clinic, confirm with the doctor as much as you can how much influence they have over the lab, and whether they can get an experienced embryologist assigned to you.

Another important role at the clinic stage is the agency. US law does not allow hospitals to also run surrogate matching, so that part is handled by agencies. For parents based mainly in China, the agency also handles things on the ground (such as the surrogate being admitted to the hospital ahead of delivery, etc.). We only ever worked with one agency, so I don't have much experience comparing multiple agencies.


### C. Embryos

* Start: clinic chosen
* End: viable embryos obtained

Once you've chosen a clinic (and doctor), the doctor will design a medical protocol based on both partners' checkup results, and you'll sign a contract. The protocol basically includes:

1. DNA testing to see whether both partners carry genes for the same known disease. As long as the two of you don't carry the same one, it's not much of a problem.
2. Using medication to raise the woman's hormone levels in order to get enough eggs.

A few things to pay attention to in the contract:

1. Who the embryos belong to if the couple (if applicable) divorces
2. Who the embryos belong to if one or both partners die
3. How long unused embryos will be frozen and the storage fees — typically around $700/year; some clinics also offer 5-year or 10-year packages
4. Because the two partners' effort and opportunity cost in having a child are unequal, consider signing a known sperm donation agreement, having it notarized locally, and placing it in the clinic's medical file.

As mentioned earlier, every woman has a baseline number of antral follicles, but in a normal menstrual cycle only one egg is released. That egg absorbs the nutrients from all the other antral follicles, matures, and is released. Egg retrieval is about getting all of your antral follicles to mature so they can be removed surgically. A follicle counts as mature when its radius exceeds a certain threshold.

Common protocols combine the following medications (the first 5 are hormones; the last 4 are used less often):

* Gonadotropins or injectable "fertility drugs" (Follistim, Gonal-F, Menopur);
* GnRH-agonists (Lupron);
* GnRH-antagonists (Ganirelix, Cetrotide);
* Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG) (Novarel, Pregnyl, Ovidrel);
* Progesterone, and in some cases, estradiol;
* Oral contraceptive pills;
* Clomiphene citrate or Letrozole;
* Steroids (e.g., testosterone, methylprednisolone, and dexamethasone);
* Low dose aspirin; and
* Other medications antibiotics, anti-anxiety or muscle relaxants).

One fairly controversial practice is that some doctors put patients on birth control pills, believing that the pill brings the development of multiple follicles closer in sync, so that uneven timing doesn't make it hard to maximize the number of eggs retrieved. There's also an obvious benefit: it makes it easier for the doctor to schedule the procedure. Generally speaking, women with a high follicle count tend to have fewer side effects. We tried both approaches and also consulted well-known OB-GYNs in China. Overall, based on our own experience, the protocol without birth control pills worked better.

The protocol typically lasts 2–3 weeks, starting on day 1–3 of the menstrual period.

![Screenshot 2023-10-07 at 4 52 42 PM](https://github.com/6m1w/Project-BabyX-Surrogacy/assets/117615110/4d87eb45-5e69-4cb4-94f5-a58d8af543af)


Basically, you give yourself a shot in the belly every day. The nurses will train you in detail on how to do the injections. It's not too hard, so don't worry. The key hormone monitored during this process is AMH, which can spike to 100x+ its normal level, and many women experience some mood effects from these hormonal changes. Guys, during this stage, you absolutely must not fight back — whatever she says or does, just take it.

After a few ultrasounds, the doctor will tell you the date of the egg retrieval procedure. The procedure itself is fairly simple: 10–15 minutes under general anesthesia. One option is single lumen vs. double lumen — that is, whether the retrieval is flushed with one tube or two. In terms of results, two tubes will certainly maximize the number of eggs retrieved, but using two tubes without causing tissue trauma takes real skill. You can discuss this with your doctor; it costs extra.

While the mom is having her egg retrieval, a nurse will take the dad to a small dark room to do your thing. There's a TV and DVDs in the room, and of course you can bring your own content. Don't rush — make it a quality shot. There's also room for lab skill in what happens afterward; if anyone's interested, I can add more on this later.

On the day of the procedure, the doctor will tell you how many eggs were retrieved in total. The retrieved eggs and sperm are then handed off from the doctor to the hospital's lab, where the lab does:

* In vitro fertilization (to create embryos)
    * More technically mature clinics use ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection)
* Embryo culture

Five days after the procedure, you'll be told how many embryos were successfully cultured. Don't get excited yet — a small sample is taken from each cultured embryo and sent to a third-party lab to check for chromosomal abnormalities (PGS testing), which rules out the worst diseases at the source. Each embryo that passes this step gets a grade, using letters a/b/c, e.g. bb+. The grade is based on some of the embryo's physical characteristics (whether the outer layer is smooth, etc.). Lower-grade embryos may have a somewhat lower chance of a successful transfer, but once the transfer succeeds, the grade has no other effect.

One thing I need to warn you about is a big pitfall we ran into ourselves: most doctors have much less influence over the lab than you'd imagine. In other words, once you get to the lab stage, it's very uncertain whether your embryos will be cultured by a newly hired junior or by the person with the highest success rate in the lab. Also, the lab skills of many doctors in the US are noticeably weaker than those of the top-trained specialists in China. So a very important part of choosing a clinic is understanding the lab's success rate. In our own case, this was extremely important.

By this point, what you have are embryos ready to be transferred into a surrogate. The doctor will also tell you the sex distribution, which varies from person to person — some people get 100% one sex, others get half and half. For the transfer, generally one embryo is transferred per surrogate, and doctors will recommend this too, to keep the risk manageable. I did think about whether I wanted to transfer two embryos into one surrogate. The upside is that the two kids would be born at the same time. The downsides: a. the risk during the process goes up significantly; b. the two kids would be **fraternal twins**, so they wouldn't look very alike.

To sum up, the embryo stage looks like this:

Choose a clinic → doctor sets the protocol → daily injections for about two weeks, with hormone monitoring along the way → doctor sets the procedure date → egg retrieval and sperm collection → lab stage → day-5 embryos are sent for PGS testing → you get embryos ready for transfer to a surrogate, which are then frozen. When you decide to do a transfer, the embryo to be transferred is thawed. Embryos can stay frozen for more than ten years, but their viability is certainly related to how fresh they are.

Let's talk about conversions.

Individual variance here is huge, and there's no good formula to summarize it. Many doctors are also reluctant to give patients specific numbers to expect. After discussions with five or six doctors, here are the conversion rates we put together based on our own experience. This is not a scientific calculation — for reference only. Taking an Asian woman around 30 years old as the mother:

| | **Antral follicles** | **Fertilized eggs** | **Day-5 embryo survival** | **PGS pass rate** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conversion rate vs. previous step | 90%–100% | 80–90% | 50% | 50% |
| When you find out | Day of procedure | Procedure day +1 | Procedure day +5 | Procedure day +12 / 14 |

So if this woman has 15 antral follicles, she'll get roughly 12–13 fertilized embryos initially, about 6–7 will survive to day 5, and about **3–4** will pass PGS testing. These 3–4 embryos will be frozen, then thawed and transferred once a surrogate has been found.

Again, this is based only on our own experience and is shared for reference. It's not a universal formula, and the variance is large.

### C+. Egg Donation

💚 The following "Egg Donation" section was contributed by my good friend K. He is a gay man who had his baby through surrogacy in 2019.

**How much do donor eggs cost? (Q1 2024)**

Asian donor eggs: US$30,000+

White donor eggs: around US$10,000 already gives you plenty of good options

Besides the usual factors such as education, appearance, and artistic or athletic talent, two more things affect the price:

1. Chinese donors cost more, because they're scarce. First, few Chinese women become donors to begin with. Second, infertile straight Chinese couples only want (or can only choose) them. So supply and demand are out of balance, and prices are high.
2. Donors with previous donation experience cost more — especially those who have produced a large number of follicles in the past.

Because of the supply-demand imbalance for Chinese donor eggs, agencies based in China have recently started recruiting egg donors in China and flying them to the US to complete the retrieval, which is also legal and has greatly increased supply. For example, a friend of mine was matched through one of these agencies with a donor who graduated from a top Chinese university (a "985/211" school; US$30,000+), and they also saw an Asian donor in the database who graduated from Columbia University (US$70,000–80,000).

**What information can you see when choosing a donor?**

Intended parents get access to a database with detailed information on each donor, including but not limited to:

- Adult and childhood photos; some even provide photos of their father and brothers, for reference on what a baby boy might look like;
- Education (GPA) and work history. How much is disclosed varies — I've seen donors fully disclose their school and employer, but most only give a general description;
- Major medical history, hereditary disease history, and surgical history — not only their own, but also that of all their siblings and the two generations above them. If anyone in the two older generations has passed away, the cause of death is listed;
- A genetic testing report, with particular attention to whether she carries DNA for any hereditary disease she may not even be aware of;
- Privacy, which comes in three levels:
    - Identified, open to contact at any time in the future;
    - Fully anonymous, no contact under any circumstances;
    - Semi-anonymous, contact allowed when there's a medical need (an extreme example being certain diseases that require a bone marrow donation from a relative);
- Data from past donations: number of follicles, number of embryos, number of successful implantations, number of successful births;
- A psychologist's evaluation report;
- And finally, a set of fun open-ended questions, like "What three things would you bring to a desert island?", "Who has had the biggest influence on your life?", "What are your favorite books and music?", "Why are you donating eggs?", and so on;
- Because lawyers, doctors, and other professionals are involved, none of the above information can be made up.

The database is updated every day. Great donors are in high demand, so I recommend refreshing it often.

For example, I was once matched with a young Jewish violinist whose motivation for donating was to buy an expensive violin. But right as we were about to sign the contract, she received a large scholarship from a top music school in London and decided not to donate after all. Later, that violinist even came to China to give a solo recital. Missing her was a pity, but it was also fate.

Unlike surrogates, donors don't have an interview stage; intended parents can only choose based on the materials. Because most donors choose to remain anonymous, intended parents can't be present when she goes to the hospital for the retrieval. But we still felt secure throughout the whole process, because the doctor reported the progress and results of the retrieval in great detail, so I had firsthand information the whole time.

**Tips for choosing**

- Be clear about your goals and priorities so you can decide quickly. Great donors are gone in an instant — hesitate, and someone else will book her.
- Your goals will be shaped by your values, social views, educational philosophy, and more, so I recommend spending enough time thinking them through before you start.
- Using myself as an example: my first goal was that her health had to be flawless; my second was that both appearance and educational background had to be at least an 80 out of 100; my third was that outstanding athletic ability could justify some compromise on the second goal.
- If your budget allows, try not to choose a first-time donor. The fee is fixed no matter how many follicles are retrieved. Charging "per cycle" makes sense, because one retrieval is one complete medical procedure. But with a first-time donor, even if all her test results look ideal, there's a risk that only a few follicles will be retrieved.

**Advice for the LGBT community**

Do LGBT couples face special challenges raising children in China? Of course. But from my personal experience, many of those challenges and fears are actually just "imagined."

In real life, first, there aren't that many difficulties, and second, there's always a way around them. My child has already finished three years of kindergarten and has tried all kinds of extracurricular classes and community activities. They're healthy and confident.

### D. The Surrogate

Start: embryos that passed PGS

End: pregnancy confirmed after the transfer

One step closer to your little one's arrival. Now it's time for a new partner to join the team. I recommend interviewing surrogates only after your embryo results are in, to avoid unnecessary financial and emotional costs.

You'll need an agency to help you find a surrogate, and the agency will play an extremely important role throughout the rest of the process. It's best to go with one recommended by a friend you trust, and have your friend make the introduction.

Of the 50 US states, only 8 recognize surrogacy as legal. Legal here means that the child's birth certificate lists the names of the DNA parents rather than the birth parent. Even in New York State, births via surrogacy are not legal. So many friends who go to clinics in New York actually have the delivery in Massachusetts, next to New York. Also, the hospital where the baby is delivered is different from the clinic where the transfer is done. Before choosing a surrogate, think about where you'd like to live during the period when you come to pick up your baby, and choose a surrogate near that location.

Your agency will send you some surrogate profiles. Pick ones you feel good about, and if there's a surrogate you like, ask the agency to set up a video call. But please understand that for most surrogates, the core motivation is financial income, so they hope every interview goes well. Failing lots of job interviews knocks anyone's confidence — it's the same for everyone. And just like job hunting, it's hard to learn everything about your surrogate — her experience, her motivation, how she became a good mother — in a 20–30 minute remote video call. Realistically, no matter how strictly the contract prohibits smoking, drinking, or getting a perm, if your surrogate secretly has a small sip of alcohol one day, you'll have no way of knowing. Of course, there are options such as living with the surrogate, but those cost more and aren't necessarily more effective.

At the same time, because surrogates are paid by milestone, they also have a strong incentive to make sure everything goes smoothly during the pregnancy.

![Screenshot 2023-10-07 at 4 53 33 PM](https://github.com/6m1w/Project-BabyX-Surrogacy/assets/117615110/02ae5c01-3671-4fb8-9f32-4fbc5726803e)


Personally, when talking with surrogates, I paid the most attention to these points:

1. Whether she has surrogacy experience. Experienced surrogates are more composed, and you can also learn her past success rate. Generally, once a woman has been pregnant 5 times, doctors advise against her becoming a surrogate.
2. Support system. The divorce rate in the US is high, so many surrogates have a boyfriend and kids but aren't legally married. That doesn't stop them from getting support from their boyfriend, of course. Even better is living close to parents and siblings, which means more support.
3. A regular job — for example, a job at a school — is like having another employer run a background check for you. It's worth asking for more detail about the job. I once talked with a surrogate who told me she worked in the beverage industry; only after asking some follow-up questions did I realize she worked at a bar.
4. Whether she has kids of her own. Parents with kids are generally more careful in daily life — no smoking, no drinking.

Surrogates with strong backgrounds are in high demand. During COVID, fewer intended parents were looking for surrogates, so you could take your time choosing. In Q3 2023, good surrogates were getting booked within a few days. After you choose a surrogate, she needs to go through a series of checks. The US process is much slower overall than in China — checks plus transfer can take anywhere from 1 to 6 months. Yes, 6 months.

Two weeks after the transfer, the surrogate will start various tests. A simple test strip can tell you whether she's pregnant, and the clinic will also do a blood test to confirm. Basically, about a month after the transfer, the pregnancy can be medically confirmed. However, there is indeed a chance that **the transfer doesn't result in a pregnancy** — we experienced this ourselves. The reasons are rather mysterious, and no one can give you a definite answer. You can try another transfer with the same surrogate; in our case, we switched to a different surrogate.

Once the pregnancy is confirmed, the surrogate moves into the regular US pregnancy track and sees her own OB (obstetrician). Ask about progress after each visit, and ask for lots of photos.

Congratulations. In another 8–9 months, you'll get to meet your little one.


### E. The Newborn

Start: 1 month before the due date

End: successfully bringing the baby back to China

This is usually the most exciting time: shopping for the baby. There are plenty of newborn gear checklists on Xiaohongshu (RedNote), so I won't repeat them here. I'll just mention two things: in the US, you absolutely need an infant car seat that's easy to take in and out. And stock up on ready-to-feed liquid formula.

If all goes well, your surrogate's checkups will all look good. At 36 weeks the baby is already full term and, strictly speaking, could be born at any time. We arrived in the US at around 37–38 weeks. For first-time parents: I recommend hiring a postpartum nanny (a "yuesao"). Besides being more experienced, the more important benefit is that she reduces direct conflict between the two of you.

The OB will let the surrogate know when the delivery will happen. You need to get to the hospital that day. Have the surrogate or the agency give the hospital a heads-up — you'll need a room of your own and will stay one night in the hospital. Soon after the baby is born, they'll be brought to your room. From then on, it's you and the nanny taking care of them. A newborn's needs are pretty simple: eat and sleep. Spend lots of time with them — they grow fast. One key thing to watch is jaundice. If the levels are too high, the baby will be sent to the ICU for phototherapy. Don't worry if this happens; monitoring and treatment are very mature nowadays. But you need to find a way to get insurance for the baby, or the extra costs can be high.

Babies can usually be discharged after 24–36 hours. Bring all the paperwork (the vaccination record and the hospital's own birth record). The most important thing before discharge: explicitly ask the hospital to submit the SSN application and the birth certificate application for you. Get the contact details of the person responsible for this.

There's not much to say about caring for the baby — still eat and sleep. Let me focus on documents. I'll assume you need the following:

* Chinese Travel Document
* US passport
* Documents for the baby's household registration (hukou) in China. Yes, big cities in China have an established process for registering children born via surrogacy who come back to China, and you don't really need to worry about being penalized later — the policy encourages newborns.

Overall, I also recommend hiring an agency that specializes in paperwork, mainly because they have better control over the timeline. But many of these agencies are mediocre and can't even clearly explain the dependencies between steps. Here's my summary:

1. The starting point for all of this is the birth certificate — the one you must ask the hospital to apply for before discharge. Only once you have it can you apply for the US passport and the Chinese Travel Document in parallel. The hospital submits the birth certificate to the county where the baby was born. A week after the birth, you can start calling the county's public health department to check on progress; it usually takes 2–3 weeks. But make sure the hospital submits the application to the county promptly. At least one parent must pick up this certificate in person (mail takes quite a while).
2. While waiting for the birth certificate, have the agency give you a DNA testing kit and get a DNA test done; this is needed for the Chinese Travel Document. I considered using a DNA test kit from CVS, but the core challenge is that this DNA test needs a lab signature plus third-party notarization. In the end, I used the lab the agency recommended.
3. Once you have the birth certificate:
    1. You can apply for the US passport. This requires an in-person appointment at USPS, which you can book in advance; have the agency prepare the forms for you.
    2. Start preparing the materials for the Chinese Travel Document. You'll need to download an official app, complete some verification, and fill in some forms; have the agency prepare these for you.
       -   Some friends asked why the travel document requires DNA testing. It's because the consulate requires prenatal checkup reports containing the mother's personal information.
    3. Three-level authentication, which is needed to register the baby's hukou back in China; have the agency handle this too.
4. After submitting the materials in step 3, you can usually get them in 3–4 weeks. So the whole process takes 5 weeks if it's fast, 7 weeks if it's slow.

Passport / hukou questions:

1. Not sensitive in the short term; in the medium to long term, it depends on relations between the two countries.
2. Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen allow hukou registration. Before our kids were born, I checked with the police station where my hukou is registered, and they have an established process for registering children born via surrogacy in the US — no special arrangements needed. Parents in other regions are welcome to add their experience.
3. There's no issue of holding two passports, because the child only has a US passport and a Chinese Travel Document. The travel document can be used until age 18, so there's no passport conflict before then. This is a tacit understanding between the two countries' embassies. The travel document must be renewed every two years.

### F. Documents for Returning to China

Taking Beijing as an example, you'll need:
- Birth certificate / three-level authentication (embassy level): the originals plus a translation from a translation company recognized by the police station (Beijing Shuangxiong)
- Both parents' hukou booklets, ID cards, marriage certificate, and property ownership certificate
- The child's travel document / the child's mother's visa (passport)

Just go to the police station and tell them the baby was born in the US; there's an existing household registration process. The documents won't mention **surrogacy**, and there won't be any corresponding record in China.

Overall, right now (Oct 2023), the attitude toward US-born babies is fairly welcoming. For a while, you had to sign a statement renouncing the US passport, but those requirements are gone now.
